Abstract
Traction electric machines in electrified mobility are constrained by limited supply volt-
age and critical materials. Active winding reconfiguration (AWR) changes the winding
connection during operation to improve voltage utilization and field-weakening (FW)
capability. However, general design rules for AWR remain limited. This work extends the
characteristic-current-based normalized parameter-plane framework to AWR via topology-
dependent reconfiguration factors and evaluates the resulting torque-speed-power behavior
over the design space. The analytical model is compared against finite-element analysis
of six baseline machines and test-bench measurements from one machine. Normalized
characteristic current is the dominant design parameter, capturing the balance between
magnetic and current loading, while saliency and the reconfiguration factor set the magni-
tude of the benefit. Figures of merit are defined to quantify reconfiguration benefit across
peak performance, crossover points, torque-speed envelopes, and part-load performance.
Across these figures of merit, three explicit thresholds for normalized characteristic current
are analytically derived and identified. Below the lowest threshold, designs such as syn-
chronous reluctance and ferrite-assisted machines gain up to four times higher constant
power speed range and 40% higher peak power, based on the analytical model. Above it
and up to the middle threshold, conventional rare-earth traction machines gain mainly in
part-load efficiency instead. Above the highest threshold, narrow-FW designs gain mainly
in speed-range extension. These thresholds provide generalized, geometry-independent
early-stage AWR screening for traction machine concepts.
Keywords: active winding reconfiguration; normalized parameter plane; characteristic
current; saliency; traction electric machine; field weakening; permanent magnet synchronous
machine; star-delta; series-parallel; winding reconfiguration; open-end winding;
1. Introduction
Primary traction electric drives for electrified mobility must balance cost, sustain-
ability, and high-speed performance under a fixed direct current (DC) link voltage con-
straint. Several rare-earth-free rotor solutions exist, including induction machines (IM),
synchronous reluctance machines (SynRM), permanent magnet (PM)-assisted SynRMs
(PMaSynRM), and externally excited synchronous machines (EESM). Each introduces
varying trade-offs with torque density, efficiency, volume, or thermal margin for reduced

critical-material content. A general survey in [1] compares EESMs with permanent magnet
synchronous machines (PMSMs) and highlights this trade-off at a broad level. Under
common stator and inverter constraints [2] shows that thermally limited EESMs achieve
lower performance than neodymium–iron–boron (NdFeB)-PMSMs. Extending the compar-
ison to ferrite-magnet PMaSynRMs and induction machines, ref. [3] shows that both are
limited to about two-thirds of the best-case NdFeB-PMSM performance. When machine
volume is not constrained, ref. [4] shows that ferrite-magnet PMaSynRMs require about
50% larger machine volume and 37% higher inverter current rating to match rare-earth
machine performance.
Active winding reconfiguration (AWR) has regained attention as a means to address
this trade-off. Early traction PMSM studies in [5] introduced star (Υ), delta (∆), and parallel
(Π) reconfiguration using thyristors and electromechanical contactors. Tapped-winding
layout using two three-phase diode bridges and two insulated-gate bipolar transistor
(IGBT) switches were introduced in [6], including an unequally split tapped-winding
topology [7], while ref. [8] proposed a combined tapped and Υ–∆topology for agricul-
tural tractor application with a symmetric torque-interrupted switching sequence. More
recently, ref. [9] applied an AWR series-parallel (Υ–Π) topology to a ferrite-PMaSynRM.
For induction machines, ref. [10] determined AWR for a single phase machine can provide
three times speed extension, while ref. [11] applied Υ–Π AWR to demonstrate three times
increased power at extended speed range, whereas, a variable stator pole machine topol-
ogy was proposed in [12]. A modeling method using winding function approach for the
analysis of transient winding reconfiguration was proposed in [13]. Υ–Π concepts with
anti-parallel thyristor modules were studied in [14]. In [9], an AWR Υ–Π topology was
applied to a rotating 80 kW machine, but reconfiguration was limited to low speed and low
torque because of high current transients. By contrast, ref. [15] demonstrated Υ–Π AWR at
moderate speed and torque representative of an automotive traction machine. While these
studies addressed low-level reconfiguration switching using symmetric winding states
during transition, asymmetric switching states were examined in [16] to minimize torque
interruption, while ref. [17] considered similar sequencing for smaller machines. Ref. [18]
proposed a fast reconfiguration control method for Υ–Π switching within a single inverter
switching period. The design proposed in [19] additionally discussed a fail-safe transition
sequence using active short circuit during switching. Thus, several works have dealt with
the wide ranging aspects around AWR design and control, and show the performance
impact of the proposed design in terms of speed, torque, power and losses improvement.
However, they remain largely at the level of single-point design demonstrations.
Reported gains in several of these studies are substantial and span a wide-range,
including 20% to 80% increases in torque or power density and up to 60% reductions
in total losses [20]. Yet, there is a lack of clear design parameters or threshold ranges to
predict this benefit without a full design study. A sensitivity analysis [21] found that
designs with weak rotor magnetic loading benefit more from AWR, albeit based on a
single rotor design. However, the trend begins to reverse at very low magnetic loading
and therefore does not generalize further. This work and a comprehensive review [20]
identified the need for research on design methodologies for machines with reconfigurable
windings from the conceptual stage. Identifying benefits currently requires a full AWR
design study. Conventional multi-objective optimization methods require numerous detail
design evaluations [22]. While data-driven approaches reduce this evaluation count [23,24],
such design studies benefit from analytical methods to further reduce computational effort.
Generalized design-space explorations have used analytical normalized parameter-
plane (NPP) models based on the flux-linkage–saliency (ΨPM −ξ) parameter-plane intro-
duced in [25]. Practical saturation effects on the analytical model were examined in [26],

ical trends experimentally. However, the boundaries identified in the NPP vary along the
input parameters, requiring iterative solving or look-up tables (LUTs) for each design [32],

In [33] the importance of characteristic current (Ich) in strongly influencing CPSR
under field-weakening (FW) is highlighted. Ref. [34] shows that, although positioning the
Ich close to the rated current maximizes FW capability, it does not improve high-speed effi-
ciency. Ref. [35] builds on this to propose an design-optimization criterion for positioning
the characteristic current to enhance high-speed efficiency region. The normalized charac-
teristic current ( ˆIch)-based NPP model introduced in [36] expresses a design-independent,
closed-form boundary at ˆIch = 1. This threshold value represents theoretical infinite CPSR
capability. Moreover, the work showed that saturation can cause large deviations of up to
50% in peak torque from the analytical prediction, while the CPSR and FW envelope exhibit
only minor deviation. Since AWR benefit is fundamentally governed by FW capability, this
type of NPP model is well suited for extension to AWR topologies, although ref. [36] did
not originally address AWR.
One study [37] extended the ˆIch based NPP model to AWR by introducing a third
parameter, normalized FW current, for series-parallel reconfiguration topology. However,
the study omitted the effect of turns ratio on PM flux linkage, as otherwise expected [28,30].
The same single threshold from [36], ˆIch = 1, is identified qualitatively, without any figure
of merit (FoM) to quantify torque or power gain over the CPSR. Validation is limited to
a design at ˆIch = 4, a machine with inherently narrow FW. The recommendation is that
machines with narrow FW (large ˆIch and low saliency) primarily benefit from AWR and
contradicts the findings in [21]. Broader case-study evidence [20] also indicate substantial
AWR benefit for machines with wide FW and CPSR capability ( ˆIch ≈1).
The gap therefore remains threefold: an AWR-extended ˆIch based NPP model that
accounts for the turns-ratio effect on PM flux linkage, expression of reconfiguration benefit
through quantified FoMs, and validation across the wide-FW region near ˆIch ≈1.
The goals of this work are:
1.
Extend the two-parameter NPP model from [36] with turns-ratio effect to obtain a
geometry-independent analytical model that predicts the torque-speed-power behav-
ior of AWR topologies over the design space.
2.
Derive a reduced set of FoMs from this model that quantify the benefit of AWR
reconfiguration.
3.
Identify explicit parameter thresholds that separate AWR-favorable from AWR-
unfavorable design regions.

The normalization of any given reference drive and reference machine is made with

2.2.2. Significance of Normalized Characteristic Current
Previous research established NPP models for describing the FW capability of IPMSM.
In this work, the formulation based on normalized characteristic current ˆIch and saliency
ratio ξ is used. The characteristic current is a fundamental machine parameter and forms a

key boundary recognized for its role in determining FW performance. In its normalized
form (i.e., ˆIch), values above one indicate that the voltage limit ellipse intersect is outside
the current limit circle, so the machine becomes speed-limited, whereas values below one
allow continued FW toward higher speed with diminishing torque. Saliency ξ modifies the
shape of the voltage-limit ellipse and therefore the effectiveness of current utilization in
FW; higher saliency reduces the d-axis current needed to remain within the voltage limit
and improves torque-per-ampere output.
The characteristic current represents the ratio of the magnet flux to the d-axis induc-
tance. When normalized against maximum stator current limit, it points to the machine’s
relative magnetic loading and current loading, both fundamental design parameters in
preliminary, analytical machine design.
The characteristic current also equals the balanced three-phase steady-state short-
circuit current of the machine at high-speeds under idealized assumptions. ˆIch placed near
the maximum current limit therefore implies that a stator short circuit alone can approach
the magnet’s demagnetizing field. Reduced- and rare-earth-free magnet designs, with
lower demagnetization resistance, thus often target ˆIch below this limit, most notably in
ferrite PMaSynRM. ˆIch thus also captures the trade-off between demagnetization resistance
and FW capability.
Together, ˆIch and ξ provide a physically grounded way to locate traction-machine
designs in the NPP. In this plane, most wide-CPSR machines, including those in automotive
traction use, typically occupy the region with characteristic current around the rated
maximum current limit (i.e., 0.8 < ˆIch < 1.2) and a saliency, ξ > 1 [34].

3.2. Crossover Point FoMs
The crossover FoMs identify where the reconfigured envelope begins to dominate the
anchor envelope and the voltage stress associated with such a mode transition. Values
of κu,x,1 above unity indicate that the open-circuit induced voltage exceeds the available
DC-link voltage. Under a short-circuit condition, this can lead to a risk of uncontrolled
generating operation if no active control is applied. In an open-circuit condition, the
resulting terminal voltage must remain below the blocking-voltage rating of the inverter
switching devices, as observed in [30].
Increasing ξ improves the anchor-state FW capability and therefore shifts the crossover
to higher speed, while the dependence on ˆIch produces a ridge near ˆIch ≈1. Below this
region, the reconfigured state extends useful high-speed operation; above it, the FW benefit

3.4. Part-Load Performance FoMs
The FoMs described so far characterize performance along the rated torque–speed
envelope only. To address part-load performance, two additional FoMs, ¯κPF and ¯κCu, are
defined based on indicative power factor (PF) and ohmic losses, respectively. Though
stator ohmic losses were neglected in the derivation of the normalized voltage and torque
equations, a representation of these losses is useful for design analysis and is post-processed
from the analytical model results [36].
Both FoMs are area-weighted means of the point wise difference between the reconfig-
ured and anchor states, normalized over the common feasible operating region Ωcommon;
positive values indicate that reconfiguration is favorable. ¯κPF captures the reduction in
apparent power demand on the inverter for a given torque and speed, while ¯κCu captures
the improvement in part-load efficiency through reduced stator ohmic (DC) loss.
Figure 7 shows ¯κPF in the base-speed region improves with ˆIch but degrades with ξ,
whereas in deep FW the trend reverses. Since Ωcommon is dominated by the base-speed
region, and predominantly by that of the reconfigured state, ¯κPF follows the base-speed
trend: it decreases with ξ and increases with ˆIch.
Reconfiguration reduces phase current in the ∆state and the resistance Reff in the
Π state. As copper loss scales with I2Reff, the advantage is largest near the current limit
and shrinks at part load. ¯κCu is further constrained by the definition of Ωcommon itself: for
ˆIch ≤1/kw,rec, a large fraction of the reconfigured envelope lies outside Ωcommon. Therefore,
the advantage is only partially captured, whereas at higher ˆIch the overlap grows and the
copper-loss reduction is more fully reflected in ¯κCu.

The baseline machines M1–M6 (Table 5) are used to compare nonlinear FEA results
against the extended NPP model at matched normalized design points. Measurement
results of machine M3 are additionally included. The original winding connection is treated
as the Υ anchor, and the ∆state is obtained by reconfiguring the FEA models and the test

4. Discussion
The relevant transition regions in the normalized design space occur at three charac-
teristic thresholds:
1.
t1 :
ˆIch ≤
1
kw,rec marks the region of maximum CPSR and torque–speed envelope
improvement.
2.
t2 : ˆIch ≈1 is where maximum-power improvement flattens and crossover voltage
stress become critical.
3.
t3 : 1 < ˆIch ≤
k3w,rec−1
kw,rec(k2w,rec−1) is where torque–speed envelope improvement is least.
Thresholds t1, t2, and t3 are analytical thresholds based on CPSR speed ratio of the
lossless, linear NPP-based formulation used in this work. Their locations are given by ˆIch
and kw,rec, whereas their locations can be offset when considering losses and saturation
(25). The FoMs themselves depend on the selected speed range, normalization base, and
common operating region, even if the thresholds are analytically fixed. For a meaningful
comparison, the evaluated speed range should cover a wide enough normalized FW region
and be referenced consistently to the corner point definition used in this framework.
In the present results, the FoMs associated with the composite CPSR, maximum-power
ratio, torque-envelope improvement, crossover speed, and voltage-stress behaviour show
characteristic contours around the threshold values. Saturation modifies the torque-current
relationships, particularly around the base-speed peak torque and crossover regions, and
can therefore influence the transition behaviour observed via FEA models of saturated
machine designs. Accordingly, the thresholds should be interpreted as analytical design-
region boundaries for normalized preliminary screening, rather than as universal physical
transition points.
The key implications are summarized in Table 8. The analytical ˆIch −ξ NPP model
shows poor to no agreement with torque and PF related FoMs, as also observed in the
grounding framework in [36]. Agreement is better for envelope, CPSR and ohmic loss
related FoMs which are valuable for determining benefits of AWR. Thus, based on these
FoMs several design recommendations can be made for automotive traction machines with
the AWR topologies considered.

relative to current-loading, which in turn, can move the machine into the low- ˆIch region

Designs with high ˆIch
For machines with high ˆIch, i.e., with high magnetic strength and low current loading

Choice between reconfiguration pairs
The absolute improvement in high speed performance and torque–speed envelope
FoMs is higher for Υ–Π reconfiguration pair than the Υ–∆pair. This follows from the larger
effective reconfiguration factor kw,rec of the Υ–Π pair. However, following aspects favor
Υ–∆pair:
1.
The crossover speed is lower for the Υ–∆pair, hence, the benefits are already available
at lower speeds.
2.
The crossover voltage-stress is lower for the Υ–∆pair, hence, the risk of mode-shift
is lower.
3.
The threshold value for CPSR and torque–speed envelope improvement is inversely
proportional to kw,rec. More machine designs are covered by the Υ–∆pair than the
Υ–Π pair.
Limitations
The present study is intentionally limited to high-level, geometry-independent, nor-
malized, scalable AWR screening for machine designs. The objective of this work is the
relative identification of AWR-favorable regions in the normalized design space before
deeper electromagnetic FEA based design space exploration.
Temperature-dependent electromagnetic and resistive properties, are neglected assum-
ing equal thermal conditions for the envelopes. In practice transient thermal conditions
influence the continuous operation capabilities and are not addressed in this study. Copper
utilization and its impact on losses are also neglected in this analytical lossless framework.
Triplen-harmonic circulating currents in the ∆connection are not represented by
the fundamental-wave d-q model, which is consistent with the present scope. In such
machines, circulating currents can represent a significant fraction of the maximum fun-
damental current rating, also impacting losses. These require dedicated voltage-source
transient simulations and measurement equipment. These are are outside the scope of the
present work.
Torque ripple is outside the present steady-state fundamental envelope analysis. How-
ever, torque ripple may differ between configurations through the saturation dependent
harmonic effects such as with delta-winding connection and requires dedicated voltage-
source, time-domain and harmonic investigation
Reconfiguration transients and inverter switching dynamics of active and online
winding reconfiguration are not considered because the model addresses steady-state
operating envelopes. Switching transients and voltage stresses during reconfiguration were
not experimentally investigated, but they remain important for implementation and control
validation and are left for future work.
5. Conclusions
This work addressed the need for a geometry-independent method to assess when
AWR is beneficial for traction PMSM designs. Overall, the three goals stated in the introduc-
tion were achieved. First, the ˆIch-based NPP model was extended to AWR by introducing
topology-dependent reconfiguration factors, kw,rec, for the Υ–∆and Υ–Π configurations,
while keeping the Υ machine as the normalization anchor.The resulting model predicts

Together they show that the benefits from AWR for a given machine type depend on

relative strength of PM flux linkage, or magnetic loading, with respect to current loading.
Comparison with nonlinear FEA results for six representative traction-machine de-
signs. Measurement results from one machine (M3) covering steady-state characteristics
without active reconfiguration switching is also compared. These confirmed that the ana-
lytical model captures the main system-level trends, including composite CPSR, envelope

presented metrics compared with the FEA model, due to the absence of nonlinear saturation
effects in the analytical model. The extended analytical NPP model is therefore suitable for
early-stage design screening and for identifying machine concepts that can benefit from
reconfiguration, while final performance prediction still requires nonlinear FEA due to
saturation-dependent inductance effects influencing several important peak-performance
and part-load loss metrics.
Overall, the proposed NPP-based framework provides a compact design-screening
tool for exploring AWR machine concepts and identifying promising regions of the design
space before detailed electromagnetic optimization.

Appendix D. FEA Modeling of the Baseline Machines
The modeling workflow is organized into three levels:
1.
2D FEA of a single operating-point: The two-dimensional cross-section of the machine
including the active parts of stator and rotor laminations, the PM, and the air gap is
modelled. Based on the number of poles and slots, geometry and winding configu-
ration, only a section (for e.g., one pole section) of the whole machine is modelled
based on assumptions of symmetry and continuity of electromagnetic fields. For
this selected section, a mesh is generated and fields are calculated (Figure A2). The
electromagnetic field solution is post-processed to determine output quantities such
as flux linkages, currents, voltages, torque, torque ripple, and losses at each simulated
operating point. Five loss mechanisms are addressed: core loss in the stator and
https://doi.org/10.3390/en19204849


===== PAGE 28 =====

Energies 2026, 19, 4849
28 of 32
rotor laminations based on modified and improved generalized Steinmetz equation
coefficient curve fitting [42,43], magnet eddy-current loss [44], ohmic losses (DC and
AC winding [45]), and friction loss. PWM switching losses and stray load losses are
neglected. Other standard assumptions for 2D FEA of rotating machinery apply :
•
Stator windings are driven by ideal balanced sinusoidal three-phase current
excitation. Supply harmonics, inverter switching effects, and control dynamics
are neglected.
•
The electromagnetic field is solved using transient 2D FEA with prescribed rotor
motion.
•
End effects and end-winding leakage inductances are not accounted for in the
2D FEA, rather they are accounted for separately.
•
Laminated core is modeled with isotropic magnetic properties and a stacking
factor of 0.97. Inter-lamination eddy currents are assumed to be negligible.
•
Fixed uniform temperature, and mechanically rigid body at each simulated
operating point.
2.
Saturation and losses LUT: FEA is carried out across the full id–iq plane at a fixed speed
matching the corresponding measurements; the resulting LUT of output quantities
are stored as scalable coefficients [46]. These are then used to evaluate results for any
other speed, current, or torque set point.
3.
Scaled models operating points sweep and losses evaluation: The method is ex-
tended to further scaling to different turns-ratio, axial length, voltage- and current-
limits [27,47].
A 2D FEA model is used here because the normalized FoMs target the steady-state
fundamental-wave torque–speed envelope and all machines are compared under identical
assumptions. It provides acceptable accuracy relative to three-dimensional FEA, while
balancing fidelity against simulation time and cost.
