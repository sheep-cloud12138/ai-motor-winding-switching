ABSTRACT This paper provides an overview of the application of reconfigurable windings in traction
motors and their prospective benefits for electric vehicles. Methods of implementation discussed are series–
parallel, star-delta, and tapped windings with series-parallel configurations having the greatest potential
for system performance improvements. The review provides valuable insights into the impact on e-drive
system mass and volume, showing significant improvements to torque and power densities of approximately
40-80% can be achieved. Other investigations targeting motor efficiency show total motor loss reductions
of approximately 30-60% are also possible. The published literature on the topic tends to be case-specific,
indicating that the generalisation of the technology applicability is lacking, with a particular need for design
tools and methodologies early in the propulsion system design stage, to explore how the technology could be
best applied. This is also highlighted by the system level impacts shown in a case study on the Nissan Leaf
drive, with series-parallel reconfigurable windings resulting in a 41.67% inverter current reduction while
maintaining baseline performance characteristics. Other key challenges to the technology uptake include
the cost and complexity of the reconfiguration device, however If these can be overcome, and the design
advantages better understood, traction drives with reconfigurable windings show potential in reducing the
size, weight, and cost of the electric machines, while improving their efficiency and power density.

significant shift towards electrification leading to a growing
demand for advanced electrical systems and components.
Electric traction drives, play a crucial role in the overall
performance and efficiency of the electric vehicle (EV), the
design of which is constantly evolving to meet the increasing
demands of the automotive sector [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. Incom-
ing regulations governing resource depletion and life cycle
emissions place a greater emphasis on the use and acquisition
of materials specific to the electric powertrain, translating

II. RECONFIGURABLE DRIVE CONCEPTS
The term reconfigurable windings refers to a capacity to alter
the armature winding connections of an electric machine.
This acts to effectively change the number of turns per phase
during operation. Primarily, this provides another dimen-
sion of flexibility and adaptability to the design as each
additional winding configuration, demonstrated in Figure 1,
expands the operating conditions for the machine [12]. The
means of reconfiguration can be achieved by subdividing
the windings of each phase into two parts and connecting
them in either Series or Parallel. Altering the termination
of the phases between Star and Delta also influences the
machine characteristics in the same manner, albeit to a
lesser extent. By combining both the Series-Parallel and
Star-Delta reconfiguration methods, as in Figure 1, four ‘bal-
anced’ configurations are possible from two winding parts
per phase [13].
To achieve multiple configurations during operation (with
a reasonable switching transient) additional reconfiguration
equipment is required, often depicted using semiconduc-
tors [10] or occasionally mechanical contractors [14] as the
switching components. An early example of the required cir-
cuit (Figure 1b) capable of 4 different winding configurations
totals 14 switching components. Because of the complexity,
and switch size necessary to carry the full phase current, such
a device is often represented as separate to the machine and
similar in size to the drive electronics. Furthermore, a greater
number of stator leads terminating outside of the machine are
also required; when considering a conventional three-phase
machine originally with 3 stator leads, the capability to switch
between Star-Delta configurations increases the required sta-
tor leads to 6, or to 12 for Series-Parallel reconfiguration, all
of which are represented later in Table 1.
For automotive traction machines, torque density is one
of the most important design parameters and is achieved by
maximising the magnetic forces between the rotor and stator
for the available design volume and or mass. The first step in
maximising the torque of an electric machine is to produce the
highest possible flux density. Beyond constructing a ‘good’
magnetic circuit and choosing high-quality magnetic mate-
rials, as many conductors as possible should be packed into
the available space providing a high slot fill factor. Secondly,
each conductor must carry as much current as possible with-
out reaching the thermal limit of themselves or surrounding
materials [15].

B. RESISTANCE AND INDUCTANCE
When changing the number of turns in an electric motor, both
phase resistance and inductance are affected proportionally to
the square of the number of turns. Therefore, if the number of
turns is halved, as when reconfiguring from series to parallel
connection, the inductance and resistance of the machine will
decrease by a factor of 4.

If each winding part is balanced and of the same resis-

currents) this is simplified further to a ratio of 4 between
series and parallel connections. A lower winding inductance
and resistance has a tendency to increase motor efficiency,
in part due to the influences on copper losses, iron losses,
and reactive power [13]. This partially provides insight as to
why an increase in torque at higher rotational speeds may be
available when changing from series to parallel configura-
tions as shown in Figure 3. This influence is highly sensitive
to specific machine and drive applications. Nevertheless,
a reduction in machine inductance and resistance is also
reflected in technology roadmaps, anticipating high speed
electric machines [16]. Equally, there are disadvantages with
excessively low inductances, namely increased harmonics
necessitating higher switching frequencies and further motor
control and stability considerations [13].
C. SATURATION
A realistic bound to the total flux in a machine is commonly
known as the saturation limit. Magnetic saturation can be
defined by an increase in mmf not producing a proportional
increase in the total flux. Applying this to equation (1.1),
it is logical that for this to be true, an increase in reluctance
must be present. This can be attributed to the properties of
the magnetic steel forming the stator and rotor. Although usu-
ally having negligible reluctance, it rapidly increases beyond
a certain flux density (commonly 1.8-2.0T) indicating that
machine is ‘saturated’. It is generally understood that oper-
ation in magnetic saturation, although possible, should be
avoided due to the increased losses and thermal load on the
machine, along with a decreasing return in output torque
and efficiency [15]. This can offer explanation as to why
an increased number of turns may not in fact produce a
proportional increase in torque. This forms a practical limit
to the desired mmf of the machine without incurring a higher
iron mass.

E. SALIENCY AND FIELD WEAKENING
Saliency refers to the asymmetry or variation in reluctance
of the rotor. It occurs when the rotor (the rotating part of
the motor) is deliberately designed to have a non-uniform
magnetic field distribution. This non-uniformity creates a
difference in the magnetic reluctance along different paths
within the motor as shown in Figure 4.
FIGURE 4. Differing rotor structures and saliency ratios where black
represents magnet material and grey is the rotor iron [13].
This reluctance is generally measured as the resultant
inductance in the stator windings in two coordinates - the
direct (d) axis, normal to the flux of the rotor’s magnetic
field, and the quadrature (q) axis, orthogonal, or 90◦electrical
degrees from the direct axis, which generates the torque [13].
As the magnet material has a similar reluctance to air, a sur-
face mounted permanent magnet (SMPM) rotor will have
negligible variance in reluctance, and because of this, is con-
sidered ‘non-salient.’
Rotor saliency primarily influences the speed range capa-
bility of an electric machine and torque produced by either the
magnets or reluctance of the rotor structure [17]. By precisely
knowing the rotor position, a negative d-axis current can
be applied to the stator windings which opposes the flux
of the machine’s magnets and, as a result, the airgap flux
can be reduced. This in turn reduces the back emf of the
motor from equation (1.3), making it possible to drive the
machine beyond base speed, a technique known as field-
or flux-weakening. Saliency ratios of Ld < Lq generally
improve field weakening performance due the reluctance
torque present from increasing d-axis currents, and therefore
are suited to higher speed operation [13], [17]. Conversely,
non-salient machines or saliency ratios of Ld >L q have a
comparably poor field weakening performance but can gen-
erally produce higher low speed torque. This can be because
of a higher airgap flux density as the magnets are on the
surface, not buried within the rotor structure, or a component
of reluctance torque is generated in addition to the mag-
netic field attraction under q-axis current (negative saliency).

A. RECONFIGURABLE WINDING METHODS
The most common reconfigurable winding methods covered
in literature have been classified as star-delta, series-parallel,
and tapped winding configurations. Suggested circuit dia-
grams are displayed in Figure 5, adapted in part from [37].
Each diagram shows how switching components can be
arranged to achieve two different configurations of the motor
windings when driven by a single inverter. For the series-
parallel example: when all the switches marked as series are
closed and switches marked parallel are open, the windings
in each phase are connected in series. When all the parallel
switches are closed and series switches opened, the windings
in each phase are connected in parallel. This logic applies to
the star-delta and tapped winding diagrams. Unless specifi-
cally desired as a feature, the switches for both configurations
should not be allowed to be closed simultaneously during
operation as this would ‘short’ the windings, bridging the
phases together and in effect magnetically lock the rotor.
Another feature of either of the three circuit diagrams
of Figure 5, is the ability to open any combination of the
switches (if the user has the required level of control).
As a result, the machine (or parts of the machine) can be
isolated from the drive and implemented as a fault tolerance
feature [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45], [46]. If a
single phase of the motor is damaged or inoperable, it can be
separately isolated from the drive by opening all c switches
on that phase. This would allow the remaining two phases
to continue to be driven, albeit with greatly reduced perfor-
mance. If all the switches are opened, the motor can also
be completely isolated from the drive, presenting a potential
fail-safe feature for issues with either the motor or inverter.
If the reader would like a review on the modification of
machine topologies for fault tolerance, the following refer-
ence explores this in more detail [46].
Of the three methods shown, the most pertinent to this
study is the series-parallel and star-delta methods as all the
copper within the motor remains ‘active’ and carries current
during both configurations. Tapped windings, despite having
merits in terms of reducing motor losses [36], [37], can be
explained as having a combined configuration: where the
all the windings are used within the motor (alike the series
configuration), and a tapped configuration: where half the
windings are open circuit, thus reducing the number of turns
per phase but not making a parallel connection which would
further reduce resistances and inductances while also increas-
ing the cross-sectional area of the conductors. Because of this,
the series-parallel and star-delta methods present a higher
potential for system performance enhancement and thus the
focus later on.

will be driven from a single inverter. If two inverters are
considered for driving the machine, a different strategy for
achieving multiple configurations can be employed. Star
and delta connections can be made by connecting the other
opposing ends of the phase windings to additional inverter
as shown in Figure 6. Such a method is more commonly
referred to as ‘open-end windings’ [36], [42], [43], [44],
[45], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51]. Despite the open-end wind-

strategies are required [36]. From a total of 8 semiconductor

B. STAR/DELTA VS SERIES/PARALLEL
Early investigations into the implementation of reconfig-
urable windings for traction drive applications [13], [20],
[55], show that the ratio of torque and speed between
series-parallel configurations is proportional to the ratio of
flux generation; the series connection achieving approxi-
mately twice the torque, but half the base speed of the
parallel connection. This ratio is greater than the star-delta
method; where the star configuration has approximately
1.73 times the torque capability than the delta configura-
tion. This demonstrates that the series-parallel method has
a greater capability for speed and torque extension. When
considering the complexity of reconfiguration equipment for
both methods however, the star-delta method requires fewer
stator leads and switching components, lowering the over-
all size and packaging requirements [56]. For the star-delta
method, access to each end of the phase windings is required
to achieve both configurations, the advantage being that these
are usually accessible on a conventional machine and can be
modified without removing the existing winding structure.
However, if increasing the flux density above the baseline
value is required this will necessitate rewinding the motor to
include a higher number of turns than before.

IV. APPROACH 1: REDUCING E-DRIVE SYSTEM MASS
AND VOLUME
This section will only cover the recent development of ‘recon-
figurable winding’ technology with the ability to change
configuration during operation. In its simplest form, the
volume of an electric machine is proportional to its power
capability [15], [57]. This means that when the power require-
ment is fixed by the application, the main consideration of the
winding topology is obtaining the desired torque and speed
range. Requirements of traction drives are characterised by
the ability to provide a very high transient torque, but also
cover a large speed range [58]. In applications where the final
drive ratio can be changed, this can be used to change the
torque speed relationship but there will still be a trade-off
between the torque and speed capability for a fixed winding
structure. Multispeed mechanical gearboxes are a possible
solution to this but introduces additional weight, losses, and
cost [55], [59]. The reconfigurable winding method offers an
electromagnetic alternative to a multispeed gearbox, without
the additional mechanical equipment. One of the mutually
agreed upon aims for implementing reconfigurable wind-
ings covered in literature is reducing the mass and volume
of the drive system (TABLE 3). A common methodology,
is to maintain the torque and power rating the motor whilst
reducing its volume and size [53], [60], [61], [62], [63].
As changing the winding configuration has shown to alter
torque density, the volume of the motor can therefore be
reduced whilst producing a similar performance. Other than
redesigning, or choosing a smaller machine, one accessible
method of achieving this is through shortening the axial
length of the machine [57], [60]. This action alone would nat-
urally result in reduced torque output, however, an increased
turns per phase indicative of the ‘low speed’ winding config-
uration reclaims this lost torque when operated with the same
phase current as before, albeit over a shorter speed range.
It has also been shown that the lower turns per phase of the
‘high speed’ winding configuration not only maintains the
power output of the baseline motor but can exceed it in certain
circumstances. To quantify the degree to which the motor
volume can be reduced, a study using finite element analysis
based on the Toyota Prius PMSM reported a 24% reduction
in axial length while maintaining peak torque and power
outputs [60]. Another study shows a volume reduction of
25% possible through the reduction in stator diameter while
increasing the peak torque values by 15% [58]. Furthermore,
theoretical limits for multiphase machines indicate that a 27%
reduction in motor volume is feasible compared to that of a
fixed configuration [57].
A. TORQUE DENSITY IMPROVEMENTS AND SPEED
RANGE EXTENSION
When implementing multiple winding configurations to
an existing design, which is most applications shown in
TABLE 3, the ratio of winding turns compared to the baseline
makes a large difference to the outcome performance of the
drive. Using the series-parallel method as an example, there
are three methods to choosing the new winding structure:
If the series configuration has the same number of turns as
the baseline (and thus the same characteristics), the paral-
lel connection will primarily extend the speed and power
capability of the motor. If the parallel configuration has the
same number of turns as the baseline configuration, the series
configuration will primarily increase the torque capability of
the motor. The third method is to design the series config-
uration to have a higher number of turns, and the parallel
configuration to have fewer turns than the baseline winding.
This method of choosing a number of turns either side of the
baseline configuration, increases the potential torque density,
speed range, and power density of the motor. Because of
this, it is the most popular method for demonstrating the
advantages of reconfigurable windings in literature.
This ability to alter the torque density of the motor also
influences other components in the drive system, of which the
possible elimination of mechanical/hydraulic transmissions
and their peripheral components show the greatest potential
not only in terms weight reduction, but volume, loss reduc-
tion, and efficiency increase of the drive unit as a whole [11],
[53], [55]. Mimicking the torque speed characteristics of a
two-speed gearbox, a motor with reconfigurable windings
lends itself to improving the viability of in wheel hub motors.
Given their radial dimensional constraints, to provide the
large tractive torque for low-speed acceleration, motors used
in the ‘hub’ of a conventional passenger vehicle are required
to be bulkier than geared their counterparts thus further
research in this area targeted to ‘‘achieve direct drive traction
motors with extremely high torque capacities and low weights
and volumes’’ [61].
Low power automotive drives that require extended torque
speed range characteristics have also been a key area of
research of this technology [56], [63], [64], [66], most notably
the development of a hybrid electric scooter that utilises
reconfigurable windings to enhance the operation of a small
motor generator [56]. Research on minimising costs in the
drive system focus on limiting or completely excluding the
operation of the motor in field weakening with the aim
to reduce materials in the inverter cables/connections and
permanent magnets in the motor. If the ability to use field
weakening is maintained, changing the winding configura-
tion will alter the operating characteristics of the motor in
this region, and as machine design is heavily influenced by its
operation in field weakening (or lack thereof) the topology of
the motor itself has scope to be revised. This therefore offers
further capacity for volume and mass reduction of not only the
motor itself, but the proportion of materials used within them
such as the permanent magnet mass and stator slot size [13],
[62] [65]. Furthermore, the influence of changing the winding
configuration may also have potential on reducing the size of
the cooling system for the motor and inverter as they may be
operating with different current densities to before [36].
The literature covered strongly suggests that the imple-
mentation of reconfigurable windings has the capability to

B. INCREASING MOTOR POWER DENSITIES
If the power output of a traction drive is limited by the current
density (A/mm2) of the motor windings, using configurations
with a lower number of turns than the baseline winding
structure allows for a significant increase in the high-speed
power density of the motor [36], [37], [52], [58], [64]. This is
because the cross-sectional area (mm2) will increase when
winding parts are connected in parallel (2 times) or delta
(1.73 times), thus the current(A) delivered to the motor can
increase by the same amount keeping the current density of
the phase windings constant. Simulations of [52] and shown
in Table 1, demonstrates that the series-parallel method using
half the half the number of turns of the baseline motor in the
parallel configuration can improve high-speed motor power
densities by 100%. Although the potential power output of
the motor increases, to achieve this inverter must be able
to deliver a higher phase current than before. Moreover, the
battery system will also be placed under a higher load, thus
the realistic implementation to increase the power of existing
drives would require higher rated components. Conversely,
this demonstrates the possibility for drastically reducing
motor sizes while maintaining the baseline output powers.
In cases where output power is limited by inverter cur-
rent, a configuration with a higher number of turns could
improve the low-speed power capability of the motor without
increasing the line/inverter current or any other modifications
to the drivetrain [55]. This can be used to better match the
power capability of the motor to the power capability of
the inverter, changing to a parallel or delta configuration to
maintain that power level at higher speeds [60]. The phase
current density in the motor does however increase in line
with the phase resistance which may push the existing satu-
ration or thermal limits of the motor design. Because of this,
the increase in power output may not be a linear correlation
and the motor would be expected to reach its thermal limit
faster.
V. APPROACH 2: IMPROVING E-DRIVE EFFICIENCY
Reconfigurable windings show promise of improving the
efficiency of the electric drive. Although multiple authors
have researched the generalised influence [13], [57], [70],
the exact benefits to be gained from changing the winding
configuration is shown to vary between motor topologies and
applications leading to a vast difference in aims and results in
literature shown in TABLE 4. This section will reflect on the
objectives and methods applied to improve drive efficiencies
through the implementation of reconfigurable windings for
traction drive applications. One of the key objectives for
improving the drive system performance is to reduce losses
in the drivetrain, with a particular attention to the transmis-
sion [12], [53], [56], [64]. The ability to alter the torque speed
relationship of the motor through winding reconfiguration
achieves a comparable characteristic of a multi-speed gear-

driveline efficiency. The exclusion of frictional losses associ-
ated with the continuously variable transmission (CVT) is one
application with the largest impact on improving driveline
efficiencies without the need of any other speed extension
method such as field weakening (incurring additional cost and
complexity to the controller) [56], [62], [64].

winding [61]. The greatest reduction in motor losses is seen

A. ADDITIONAL LOSSES
For the star-delta, series-parallel and tapped winding meth-
ods, current will be carried across parallel phase windings.
Depending on the placement of these parallel windings and
imperfections in the winding of the motor, one may see a
higher flux than the other. In this circumstance, due difference
in emf generated by the coils, a current will be induced
between them when a voltage is applied across the phase
terminals from the driver generating unwanted losses in the
windings [77]. Admittedly, these losses are usually negligi-
ble although research undertaken quantifying the effect of
circulating currents in parallel windings state that they can
become significant in high-speed machines. Luckily it has
been shown that by twisting the parallel windings together the
difference in phase amplitude in the windings is drastically
reduced and with it the circulating currents in them [77]. Delta
configurations reportedly suffer the most from third harmonic
currents with the adoption of non-salient rotors essential to
mitigate this issue [69].
As phase current will be carried through any reconfig-
uration equipment, any additional resistances due to the
switching components or cabling will be a source of ohmic
losses. If semiconductors are used as the switching compo-
nents, the associated losses follow the general equation of:
P = VTOI + rI2
where P is the power losses, VTO is the semiconductor
turn on voltage, I is the phase current and r is the on-state
resistance. [20].
The advantages of using semiconductor switches is the
ease of implementation, lack of maintenance, and wide
control flexibility [14]. Mechanical switching devices on
the other hand can be designed to have practically neg-
ligible losses [20], [69], have high temperature tolerances
and can be easily manufactured but also have a longer
associated switching transient where motor torque will be
interrupted.

VI. FURTHER CONSIDERATIONS
Although the implementation of reconfigurable windings
show promise an improved high-speed performance and effi-
ciency for the machine, there are a few considerations that do
not directly fall within scope of improving torque and power
density or loss reduction.
A. COST REDUCTION
RW machines with the capacity to generate higher flux den-
sities result in a lower rated current for the same torque
output [36]. A reduction in phase current allows for downsiz-
ing of semiconductor materials [73], cables motor windings,
and other ‘phase current’ rated components resulting in cost
savings in addition to overall operational costs from lower
energy consumption [36]. There is also speculation as to
whether this saving in inverter semiconductor materials could
offset the additional cost of the RW switchgear [58]. Fur-
thermore, as the motor and inverter can be operated at lower
currents, a reduction in heat can be expected further contribut-
ing to lowering system costs if the cooling system can be
simplified [36].

C. TORQUE INTERRUPTION
With the severity of delay depending on the switching archi-
tecture employed, torque interruption when changing from
one configuration to another is a common trait inherent of
the winding reconfiguration method and an area of further
research [51], [76]. All methods covered in the literature
require all phase currents to be zero during the switch-
ing event to avoid large destructive voltage spikes in the
switching assembly [53], [64]. The focus in literature dis-
cussing this torque interruption is to reduce the switching
event as much as possible to minimise the discomfort of
the vehicle passengers [20], [69]. An early example of this

VII. APPLYING RECONFIGURABLE WINDINGS TO
REDUCE INVERTER CURRENT: A CASE STUDY
The 2015 Nissan Leaf represents a well-established electric
vehicle on the market today along with a well-developed
propulsion system, the architecture of which can be gener-
alised to most existing passenger vehicles. It is because of
this, and the freely available information on the motor and
inverter, that is has been chosen to showcase a potential appli-
cation of reconfigurable windings to an existing architecture,
following a mixture of approaches 1 and 2 covered in this
review. In electric vehicles, the inverter plays a vital role in
converting DC power from the battery into a suitable wave-
form to drive the electric motor. The sizing and specifications
of the inverter are required to meet the torque and power
requirements of the motor(s). It is observed that for the pow-
ertrain of the Nissan Leaf, the combination of motor and drive
necessitates a high peak inverter current rating compared with
the peak power demand of the vehicle. This apparent over
sizing of the inverter is to satisfy the peak torque rating of the
motor, as the motor demands a significantly elevated current
from the inverter in this operating range.
The following section will briefly discuss the applica-
tion of series-parallel winding configurations to the Nissan
leaf traction motor to reduce the current demand from the
inverter: The proposed design maintains the baseline number

load and high-speed operation. The series configuration will
therefore double the effective turns per phase increasing the
ampere-turns by a factor of 2. Modelling and subsequent

turns per phase. Two independent models were developed to
represent the ‘series’ and ‘parallel’ configurations and were

IX. CONCLUSION
A full review of reconfigurable windings for traction drives
has identified three key areas for consideration when evaluat-
ing their benefits: their influence on reducing e-drive system
mass and volume, their role in improving e-drive efficiency,
and methodologies for implementation.
Studies that directly discussed improvements in motor
performance commonly found machine torque and power
density improvements of 40-80% when implementing recon-
figurable windings to existing machine designs. Other studies
targeting efficiency achieved total motor loss reductions of
30-60%. A new case study on the Nissan Leaf drive high-
lights yet other potential impacts on the drive system as
a whole, achieving a reduction in inverter current require-
ment of 41.67% while maintaining baseline performance
characteristics.
Studies that discuss the reduction in motor losses very
rarely consider system impacts, such as the influence on the
inverter or losses incurred within the switching architecture.
For example, many studies have used semiconductor-based
implementations to reduce the transient switching (recon-
figuration) time without considering the added on-state
resistance, which may negate overall system efficiency ben-
efits in some applications.
Mechanical switching components offer an attractive solu-
tion for automotive traction applications due to their sim-
plicity and low material cost compared to semiconductors.
However they are rarely explored in the literature due to
their higher transient switching time. Future work consider-
ing mechanical switches for reconfigurable windings must
ensure that the contact resistance of the switch is low enough
as to retain total system efficiency benefits.
Overall, the general applicability of reconfigurable wind-
ings is unclear due to the reliance of previous literature on
specific case studies and retrofitted machines. There is a need
for an overarching methodology for the implementation of
this technology in the early stages of machine design, and
structured analysis to identify which applications may benefit
the most.
